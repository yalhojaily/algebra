<Callout type="why">
A map grid, a chessboard, a spreadsheet, a pixel on your screen — all of them locate something with **two numbers**. In algebra we do the same with the coordinate plane, and that lets us *draw* equations. A picture of an equation shows patterns at a glance that a table of numbers hides.
</Callout>

## The coordinate plane

Two perpendicular number lines cross at their zeros. The horizontal one is the **$x$-axis**, the vertical one is the **$y$-axis**, and the point where they cross is the **origin**, $(0, 0)$.

Every point is named by an **ordered pair** $(x, y)$:
- the **first coordinate** $x$ says how far to move **left or right** from the origin;
- the **second coordinate** $y$ says how far to move **up or down**.

Click anywhere to plot a point.

<Callout type="warning" title="Order matters">
$(3, 5)$ and $(5, 3)$ are different points. Remember the alphabet: $x$ comes before $y$, and you walk **across** before you climb **up**.
</Callout>

## Quadrants

The axes divide the plane into four **quadrants**, numbered counter-clockwise starting at the upper right:

<table class="nice">
  <thead><tr><th>Quadrant</th><th>Signs</th><th>Example</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>I (upper right)</td><td>$(+, +)$</td><td>$(4, 2)$</td></tr>
    <tr><td>II (upper left)</td><td>$(-, +)$</td><td>$(-4, 2)$</td></tr>
    <tr><td>III (lower left)</td><td>$(-, -)$</td><td>$(-4, -2)$</td></tr>
    <tr><td>IV (lower right)</td><td>$(+, -)$</td><td>$(4, -2)$</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

Points on an axis, like $(0, 5)$ or $(-3, 0)$, are not in any quadrant.

## Solutions of equations in two variables

An equation like $y = 2x - 1$ has **two** variables, so a solution is a **pair** of numbers: an $x$-value and a $y$-value that together make the equation true.

## Summary

- An ordered pair $(x, y)$: move $x$ left/right, then $y$ up/down from the origin.
- Quadrants I–IV go counter-clockwise from the upper right; points on axes are in no quadrant.
- A solution of an equation in two variables is an ordered pair that makes it true. All the solutions together form its **graph**.
