<Callout type="why">
The Sun is about $149{,}600{,}000$ km away. A virus is about $0.0000001$ m wide. Counting zeros in numbers like these is slow and error-prone. Scientific notation — $1.496 \times 10^8$ and $1 \times 10^{-7}$ — makes them readable, and the exponent rules make them easy to calculate with.
</Callout>

## Raising powers to powers

The "Power rule" tab shows it: $(x^3)^2 = x^3 \cdot x^3 = x^6$ — **two groups of three** factors.

<ExponentLab client:visible />

<Callout type="tip" title="Three power rules">
$$(a^m)^n = a^{mn} \qquad (ab)^n = a^n b^n \qquad \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n}$$

To raise a product or quotient to a power, raise **every factor** to that power.
</Callout>

  client:visible

  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't forget the number">
$(3x^2)^2 = 9x^4$, not $3x^4$. The coefficient is a factor too, so it gets squared.
</Callout>

## Scientific notation

<Callout type="definition" title="Scientific notation">
A number is in scientific notation when it is written as

$$M \times 10^n, \qquad 1 \le M < 10, \;\; n \text{ an integer}$$
</Callout>

Multiplying by $10^n$ just moves the decimal point $n$ places: right for positive $n$, left for negative $n$.

## Summary

- $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$, $\;(ab)^n = a^nb^n$, $\;(a/b)^n = a^n/b^n$.
- Scientific notation: $M \times 10^n$ with $1 \le M < 10$.
- Large numbers have positive exponents; numbers between 0 and 1 have negative exponents.
