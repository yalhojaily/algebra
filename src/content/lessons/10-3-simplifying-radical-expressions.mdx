<Callout type="why">
$\sqrt{72}$ and $6\sqrt{2}$ are the same number, but $6\sqrt{2}$ tells you much more: it's a bit more than $6 \times 1.4 \approx 8.5$. **Simplified form** is the standard way to write exact answers — the diagonal of a $5$-cm square is $5\sqrt{2}$ cm — and in 10.4 it is what lets you spot like radicals and combine them.
</Callout>

## The product rule — and why it works

$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} \qquad \sqrt[3]{ab} = \sqrt[3]{a}\cdot\sqrt[3]{b} \qquad (a, b \ge 0 \text{ for square roots})$$

Why? Square the right side: $\left(\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}\right)^2 = \left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2\left(\sqrt{b}\right)^2 = ab$. So $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ is a nonnegative number whose square is $ab$ — exactly what $\sqrt{ab}$ means.

To simplify, write the radicand as a **perfect square** (or cube) times what's left, and take the root of the perfect part:

## Variables under the radical

Assuming the variables are nonnegative, an **even** power comes out of a square root with **half** its exponent: $\sqrt{x^2} = x$, $\sqrt{x^4} = x^2$, $\sqrt{x^{10}} = x^5$. An **odd** power leaves one factor inside: $\sqrt{x^5} = \sqrt{x^4}\sqrt{x} = x^2\sqrt{x}$. For cube roots, take out multiples of $3$.

  steps={[

    { show: '$= 5x\\sqrt{2x}$', why: '' },
  ]}
/>

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="A cube root with variables"
  problem="Simplify $\;\sqrt[3]{16y^5}$."
  steps={[
    { show: '$= \\sqrt[3]{8y^3 \\cdot 2y^2}$', why: '$8y^3 = (2y)^3$ is a perfect cube; $y^5 = y^3 \\cdot y^2$.' },
    { show: '$= 2y\\sqrt[3]{2y^2}$', why: '' },
  ]}
/>

## Multiplying radicals

Read the product rule the other way: $\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ — then simplify. Coefficients multiply with coefficients.

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="Multiply, then simplify"
  problem="Multiply: $\;\sqrt{6}\cdot\sqrt{10}\;$ and $\;3\sqrt{2}\cdot 4\sqrt{6}$."
  steps={[
    { show: '$\\sqrt{6}\\cdot\\sqrt{10} = \\sqrt{60} = \\sqrt{4 \\cdot 15} = 2\\sqrt{15}$', why: '' },
    { show: '$3\\sqrt{2}\\cdot 4\\sqrt{6} = 12\\sqrt{12} = 12 \\cdot 2\\sqrt{3} = 24\\sqrt{3}$', why: 'Outside numbers times outside numbers ($3 \\cdot 4$), radicands times radicands ($2 \\cdot 6$).' },
  ]}
/>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Not using the largest perfect square">
$\sqrt{72} = 2\sqrt{18}$ is true but unfinished: $18 = 9 \cdot 2$, so keep going to $6\sqrt{2}$. Using the largest perfect square gets there in one step.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Splitting a sum">
The product rule is about **products**: $\sqrt{9 + 16} \neq \sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16}$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Odd powers">
$\sqrt{x^5} = x^2\sqrt{x}$, not $x^{5/2}$ left inside or $x^2$ alone. Split off the largest even power.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Mixing up inside and outside">
In $3\sqrt{2}\cdot 4\sqrt{6}$, the $3$ and $4$ multiply each other; they never go under the radical.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$, because $(\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b})^2 = ab$.
- Simplify by taking out the largest perfect-square (perfect-cube) factor; prime factors help: each pair (triple) comes out once.
- Even powers of variables come out of square roots with half the exponent.
- Multiply radicals: coefficients with coefficients, radicands with radicands, then simplify.
