<Callout type="why">
To add $\frac{5}{6} + \frac{3}{8}$ you need a common denominator, and the smallest one keeps the arithmetic easy. That smallest common denominator is the least common multiple of 6 and 8. The same idea gives the LCD of rational expressions in Chapter 6 and answers "when will these two things line up again?" questions.
</Callout>

## Multiples and common multiples

The **multiples** of a number are what you get by multiplying it by $1, 2, 3, \ldots$:

- Multiples of 6: $6, 12, 18, \mathbf{24}, 30, 36, 42, \mathbf{48}, \ldots$
- Multiples of 8: $8, 16, \mathbf{24}, 32, 40, \mathbf{48}, \ldots$

Numbers in both lists are **common multiples**. The smallest is the **least common multiple**: $\text{LCM}(6, 8) = 24$.

## Using prime factorizations

Listing gets slow for numbers like 84 and 90. Instead, write each number as a product of primes. The LCM must contain each number's factorization, so it takes **every prime that appears, to the highest power that appears**.

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="Prime-factorization method"
  problem="Find the LCM of 84 and 90."
  steps={[
    { show: '$84 = 2^2 \\cdot 3 \\cdot 7 \\qquad 90 = 2 \\cdot 3^2 \\cdot 5$', why: 'Factor each number into primes (a factor tree helps).' },
    { show: '$\\text{primes: } 2, 3, 5, 7$', why: 'Collect every prime that appears in either number.' },
    { show: '$2^2 \\cdot 3^2 \\cdot 5 \\cdot 7$', why: 'Use the highest power of each: $2^2$ (from 84), $3^2$ (from 90), $5$, $7$.' },
    { show: '$\\text{LCM}(84, 90) = 4 \\cdot 9 \\cdot 5 \\cdot 7 = 1260$', why: '' },
  ]}
/>

In a Venn diagram of prime factors, the LCM is the product of **everything** in the picture — shared primes counted once.

## The LCM of three numbers

The same rule works for any number of numbers: every prime, highest power.

## LCM in problems

"When will things happen at the same time again?" is an LCM question. So is "what is the smallest number of items that can be split evenly into packs of $a$ or packs of $b$?"

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Always multiplying the numbers.** $6 \cdot 8 = 48$ is a common multiple, but not the least. The product is the LCM only when the numbers share no factors (like 4 and 9).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Taking the lowest power.** For the LCM use the *highest* power of each prime. Taking the lowest powers of the shared primes gives the GCD instead (see [A.3](../a-3-greatest-common-divisor-gcd/)).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Dropping primes that appear in only one number.** In $\text{LCM}(84, 90)$, the 7 comes only from 84 and the 5 only from 90 — both must be included.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A common multiple is divisible by every number; the LCM is the smallest one.
- Listing: go through multiples of the largest number until the others divide it.
- Primes: take every prime that appears, raised to the highest power that appears.
- The least common denominator of fractions is the LCM of the denominators.
