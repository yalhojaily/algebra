<Callout type="why">
The equation $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution. Mathematicians invented a number $i$ with $i^2 = -1$ — and it turned out to be indispensable: electrical engineers describe alternating current with complex numbers, and they appear throughout signal processing and quantum physics. In Chapter 11 they will let **every** quadratic equation have solutions.
</Callout>

## The number i

$$i = \sqrt{-1}, \qquad i^2 = -1$$

$i$ is not a real number — no real number squares to $-1$ — so it extends the number system, the way negative numbers once extended the counting numbers. With it, for $p > 0$:

$$\sqrt{-p} = \sqrt{-1}\cdot\sqrt{p} = i\sqrt{p}, \qquad \text{so } \sqrt{-49} = 7i \text{ and } \sqrt{-20} = i\sqrt{20} = 2i\sqrt{5}.$$

A **complex number** has the form $a + bi$ with $a$ and $b$ real: $a$ is the **real part**, $b$ the **imaginary part**. Real numbers are complex numbers with $b = 0$. Type answers like `3 - 2i`.

## Powers of i

<ComplexPlane client:visible />

$$i^1 = i,\quad i^2 = -1,\quad i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i,\quad i^4 = (i^2)^2 = 1,$$

and then the pattern repeats, because $i^5 = i^4 \cdot i = i$. To simplify $i^n$, divide $n$ by $4$: only the remainder matters.

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="A high power"
  problem="Simplify: $\;i^{23}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$23 = 4 \\cdot 5 + 3$', why: 'Divide by $4$.' },
    { show: '$i^{23} = (i^4)^5 \\cdot i^3 = 1 \\cdot (-i) = -i$', why: '' },
  ]}
/>

<QuickCheck
  client:visible
  question="Simplify $\;i^{10}$."
  options={['$-1$', '$1$', '$i$', '$-i$']}
  answer={0}
  explain="$10 = 4 \cdot 2 + 2$, so $i^{10} = i^2 = -1$."
  wrong={{ 1: '$1$ comes from exponents that are multiples of $4$. $10$ leaves remainder $2$.', 2: 'Remainder $1$ would give $i$; $10 \\div 4$ leaves remainder $2$.', 3: 'Remainder $3$ would give $-i$; $10 \\div 4$ leaves remainder $2$.' }}
/>

## Dividing

The **conjugate** of $a + bi$ is $a - bi$, and $(a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 - b^2i^2 = a^2 + b^2$ — a **real** number. So, just like rationalizing in 10.5, multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator.

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="Divide"
  problem="Divide: $\;\dfrac{5 + i}{1 - i}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{(5 + i)(1 + i)}{(1 - i)(1 + i)}$', why: 'Multiply by the conjugate $1 + i$ over itself.' },
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{5 + 6i + i^2}{1 - i^2} = \\dfrac{4 + 6i}{2}$', why: '$i^2 = -1$ on top and bottom.' },
    { show: '$= 2 + 3i$', why: 'Divide both parts by $2$ to get the form $a + bi$.' },
  ]}
/>

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="A pure-imaginary denominator"
  problem="Divide: $\;\dfrac{3}{2i}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{3}{2i}\\cdot\\dfrac{i}{i} = \\dfrac{3i}{2i^2} = \\dfrac{3i}{-2}$', why: 'Multiplying by $\\frac{i}{i}$ is enough here.' },
    { show: '$= -\\dfrac{3}{2}i$', why: '' },
  ]}
/>

## In a real context

In AC circuits, the "resistance" of a part is a complex number called its **impedance** $Z$ (ohms). Impedances in series **add**, and Ohm's law $V = IZ$ (voltage = current × impedance) **multiplies** complex numbers.

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="A circuit"
  problem="Two parts with impedances $Z_1 = 3 + 4i$ and $Z_2 = 2 - i$ ohms are connected in series. A current of $I = 2 + i$ amps flows through a part with impedance $Z = 3 - 2i$. Find the total series impedance and the voltage across $Z$."
  steps={[
    { show: '**Series:** $Z_1 + Z_2 = (3 + 4i) + (2 - i) = 5 + 3i$ ohms', why: 'Add real parts and imaginary parts.' },
    { show: '**Voltage:** $V = (2 + i)(3 - 2i) = 6 - 4i + 3i - 2i^2$', why: 'FOIL.' },
    { show: '$= 6 - i + 2 = 8 - i$ volts', why: '$-2i^2 = 2$.' },
    { show: '**State.** The total impedance is $5 + 3i$ ohms and the voltage is $8 - i$ volts.', why: 'Engineers read the real and imaginary parts as the parts of the voltage that are in step and out of step with the current.' },
  ]}
/>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="i² = 1">
$i^2 = -1$. In $(2 + 3i)(4 - i)$, the term $-3i^2$ becomes $+3$, not $-3$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="√a · √b with negative radicands">
$\sqrt{-4}\cdot\sqrt{-9}$ is **not** $\sqrt{36} = 6$. Write each in terms of $i$ first: $2i \cdot 3i = -6$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Subtracting only the first part">
$(3 + 2i) - (5 - 4i) = -2 + 6i$: the minus sign applies to $-4i$ too.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Leaving the answer unfinished">
$\frac{4 + 6i}{2}$ should become $2 + 3i$, in the form $a + bi$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $i = \sqrt{-1}$, $i^2 = -1$; $\sqrt{-p} = i\sqrt{p}$ — convert before multiplying roots.
- Add and subtract real and imaginary parts separately; multiply with FOIL and $i^2 = -1$.
- Powers of $i$ cycle with period 4: use the remainder of $n \div 4$.
- Divide using the conjugate of the denominator, then write the answer as $a + bi$.
