<Callout type="why">
Bacteria in a dish double every hour: after $h$ hours there are $2^h$ times as many. After $3$ more hours *on top of* $5$ hours, there are $2^5 \cdot 2^3$ times as many. And $2$ hours *before* we started? That needs $2^{-2}$. The rules in this lesson make calculations like these instant — and every one of them comes from simply writing out the factors.
</Callout>

## Exponential notation, and exponents 0 and 1

In $a^n$, the **base** $a$ is used as a factor $n$ times: $a^4 = a \cdot a \cdot a \cdot a$. The exponent applies only to the base right before it: $-3^2 = -(3 \cdot 3) = -9$, but $(-3)^2 = 9$.

Why is $a^0 = 1$? Follow the pattern: $2^3 = 8$, $2^2 = 4$, $2^1 = 2$ — each step down **divides by 2**. One more step gives $2^0 = 2 \div 2 = 1$. (Another way to see it: $\frac{a^3}{a^3} = 1$, and the quotient rule below gives $a^{3-3} = a^0$.)

<Callout type="definition" title="Exponents of 1 and 0">
$$a^1 = a \qquad\qquad a^0 = 1 \;\;\text{(for } a \ne 0\text{)}$$
</Callout>

## The product and quotient rules

Write out the factors and the rules appear by themselves:

$$x^3 \cdot x^2 = (x \cdot x \cdot x)(x \cdot x) = x^5 \qquad\qquad \frac{x^5}{x^2} = \frac{x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot \cancel{x} \cdot \cancel{x}}{\cancel{x} \cdot \cancel{x}} = x^3$$

Pick a rule and change the exponents:

<ExponentLab client:visible />

<Callout type="tip" title="Product rule and quotient rule">
For any number $a$ (with $a \ne 0$ when dividing) and integers $m$, $n$:

$$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \qquad\qquad \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$$

Multiplying powers of the **same base**: **add** exponents. Dividing: **subtract** them.
</Callout>

  steps={[

    { show: '(b) $\\;5 \\cdot (-2) \\cdot y^{3+8} = -10y^{11}$', why: 'Multiply the coefficients; add the exponents.' },
    { show: '(c) $\\;t^{9-4} = t^5$', why: 'Same base: subtract the exponents.' },

## Negative exponents

Continue the pattern below $2^0 = 1$: each step down divides by $2$, so $2^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}$, $2^{-2} = \frac{1}{4}$, $2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8}$. A negative exponent means a **reciprocal**. (The "Negative exponents" tab above shows the same thing.)

<Callout type="definition" title="Negative exponents">
For any non-zero $a$ and integer $n$:

$$a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} \qquad\qquad \frac{1}{a^{-n}} = a^n$$
</Callout>

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="Rewrite with positive exponents"
  problem="Rewrite: $\;3^{-2},\;\; 4y^{-3},\;\; \dfrac{1}{x^{-4}}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$3^{-2} = \\dfrac{1}{3^2} = \\dfrac{1}{9}$', why: 'A positive number — not $-9$.' },
    { show: '$4y^{-3} = \\dfrac{4}{y^3}$', why: 'Only $y$ has the negative exponent, so only $y$ moves.' },
    { show: '$\\dfrac{1}{x^{-4}} = x^4$', why: 'A negative exponent in the denominator moves up and becomes positive.' },
  ]}
/>

<StepReveal
  client:visible

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Multiplying the bases">
$2^3 \cdot 2^4 = 2^7$, **not** $4^7$: the base stays the same. And $x^3 \cdot y^4$ can't be combined at all — the bases are different.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Multiplying the exponents">
$x^3 \cdot x^4 = x^7$, not $x^{12}$. Count the factors: three plus four.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="A negative exponent is not a negative number">
$2^{-3} = \frac{1}{8}$, not $-8$. And in $5x^{-2} = \frac{5}{x^2}$, only the $x$ moves — the exponent belongs to $x$, not to $5$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $a^n$ is $n$ factors of $a$; $a^1 = a$, $a^0 = 1$ ($a \ne 0$), $a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n}$ — all from the "divide by $a$ each step down" pattern.
- **Product rule:** $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$. **Quotient rule:** $\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$. Same base only.
- Final answers are usually written with **positive** exponents.
