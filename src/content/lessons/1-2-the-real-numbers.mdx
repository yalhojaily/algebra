<Callout type="why">
On a desert night in January, the temperature might drop to $8^\circ$. On a winter night in Moscow it might be **$15^\circ$ below zero**. A diver goes **$30$ m below** sea level, and a phone battery charges to **$\tfrac{3}{4}$** full. A circle's circumference is its diameter times **$\pi$**.

Counting numbers alone can't describe all of this. In Lesson 1.1 the variables stood for numbers — now you'll meet *every kind* of number they can stand for.

## Natural numbers, whole numbers, and integers

A **set** is a collection of objects. Sets are often written by listing their members between braces, with "$\ldots$" meaning *the pattern continues forever*.

  <thead><tr><th>Set</th><th>Members</th><th>Think of it as</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Natural numbers</strong></td><td>$\{1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\}$</td><td>The numbers you count with.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Whole numbers</strong></td><td>$\{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$</td><td>Natural numbers, plus zero.</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Integers</strong></td><td>$\{\ldots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$</td><td>Whole numbers and their **opposites**.</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

On a number line, the integers are equally spaced. Numbers to the right of $0$ are **positive**; numbers to the left are **negative**. Zero is neither. Two numbers the same distance from $0$ on opposite sides, like $3$ and $-3$, are called **opposites**.

<Callout type="tip" title="Negative numbers describe the “other direction”">
Any quantity that can go two ways — above or below sea level, profit or loss, warmer or colder, deposit or withdrawal — can be described with integers. Pick one direction as positive, and the other direction becomes negative.
</Callout>

## Rational numbers

Between the integers there are many more numbers: $\tfrac{1}{2}$, $-\tfrac{3}{4}$, $2.75$, $0.333\ldots$ These are **rational numbers** — the word comes from *ratio*.

<Callout type="definition" title="Rational number">
A **rational number** is any number that can be written as $\dfrac{a}{b}$, where $a$ and $b$ are integers and ${b \ne 0}$.

Every integer is rational, because it can be written over $1$: $\;-5 = \dfrac{-5}{1}$.
</Callout>

### Fractions as decimals

Every rational number can be written as a decimal by **dividing the numerator by the denominator**. Something surprising happens: the decimal either **terminates** (stops) or **repeats** forever in a block. A bar over the digits shows the repeating block: $0.1666\ldots = 0.1\overline{6}$.

## Irrational numbers and the real numbers

Some numbers on the number line can **not** be written as a ratio of integers. Their decimals go on forever **without** repeating. These are the **irrational numbers**.

- $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots$ (the diagonal of a $1 \times 1$ square)
- $\pi = 3.14159265\ldots$ (circumference ÷ diameter of any circle)
- $\sqrt{n}$ for any whole number $n$ that is **not** a perfect square, such as $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{10}$, $\sqrt{50}$

<Callout type="definition" title="Real numbers">
The **real numbers** are all the rational numbers together with all the irrational numbers. Every point on the number line is a real number, and every real number is a point on the number line.
</Callout>

The sets fit inside each other like boxes. Pick a number and see every set it belongs to.

<NumberSets client:visible />

<Callout type="warning" title="Simplify before you classify">
$\sqrt{16}$ looks irrational, but $\sqrt{16} = 4$ — it's a natural number. Likewise $\dfrac{12}{3} = 4$ is an integer, even though it's written as a fraction. Always simplify first.
</Callout>

## Order on the number line

Real numbers are **ordered**: for any two different numbers, one is greater. The number line makes this easy.

<Callout type="tip" title="The one rule">
The number farther to the **right** is the **greater** number.

$a < b$ means "$a$ is less than $b$" ($a$ is to the left). $\;a > b$ means "$a$ is greater than $b$" ($a$ is to the right). The symbol always **points at the smaller number**.
</Callout>

Drag the two points. Pay attention to what happens when both are negative.

<NumberLineLab client:visible />

<Callout type="warning" title="Negative numbers trip everyone up">
$-9$ looks "bigger" than $-2$ because $9 > 2$. But on the number line $-9$ is farther **left**, so $\;-9 < -2$. Think of temperatures: $-9^\circ$ is colder than $-2^\circ$.
</Callout>

### Two ways to write the same fact

Every inequality can be flipped around: $\;-3 < 5\;$ says exactly the same thing as $\;5 > -3$. When you swap the two sides, you also reverse the symbol.

The symbols $\le$ ("less than **or equal to**") and $\ge$ ("greater than **or equal to**") are true if *either* part is true. So $\;4 \le 4\;$ is true, and $\;-1 \ge 3\;$ is false.

## Absolute value

How far is a number from $0$? That distance is its **absolute value**, written with vertical bars: $\lvert x \rvert$.

<Callout type="definition" title="Absolute value">
The **absolute value** of a number is its distance from $0$ on the number line. A distance is never negative, so $\lvert x \rvert \ge 0$ for every real number $x$.

$$
\lvert 7 \rvert = 7 \qquad \lvert -7 \rvert = 7 \qquad \lvert 0 \rvert = 0 \qquad \left|-\tfrac{2}{3}\right| = \tfrac{2}{3}
$$
</Callout>

Turn on "Show distance from 0" in the number line above to see absolute value as the length of an arc.

## Watch out for these

<Callout type="warning" title="Five mistakes to avoid">
1. **Ordering negatives by size of the digits.** $-8 < -3$, because $-8$ is farther left.
2. **Calling every fraction "not an integer."** $\tfrac{10}{2} = 5$ is an integer. Simplify first.
3. **Calling every square root irrational.** $\sqrt{36} = 6$. Only non-perfect squares give irrational roots.
4. **Thinking a long decimal must be irrational.** $\tfrac{1}{7} = 0.\overline{142857}$ repeats, so it's rational.
5. **Making absolute value negative.** $\lvert -5 \rvert = 5$. But $-\lvert 5 \rvert = -5$, because the minus sign is outside the bars.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Natural** $\subset$ **whole** $\subset$ **integers** $\subset$ **rational** $\subset$ **real**. The **irrational** numbers are the real numbers that are not rational.
- A **rational number** can be written as $\frac{a}{b}$ with integers $a, b$ and ${b \ne 0}$. Its decimal **terminates** or **repeats**. It terminates exactly when the simplified denominator has no prime factors other than $2$ and $5$.
- An **irrational number** has a decimal that never ends and never repeats: $\sqrt{2}$, $\pi$.
- On the number line, **right is greater**. $a < b$ and $b > a$ say the same thing.
- **Absolute value** $\lvert x \rvert$ is the distance from $0$, so it is never negative.
