<Callout type="why">
$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ are the same number. The second form is the standard one: it's easier to estimate ($1.414 \div 2 \approx 0.707$, versus dividing by $1.414$ in your head), easier to compare with other answers, and the form you'll see in textbooks and on tests.
</Callout>

## The quotient rule

$$\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} \qquad \frac{\sqrt[3]{a}}{\sqrt[3]{b}} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{a}{b}} \qquad (a \ge 0,\ b > 0)$$

It works for the same reason as the product rule: $\left(\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}\right)^2 = \frac{a}{b}$. Use it in either direction — whichever makes the numbers simpler.

## Rationalizing one-term denominators

To **rationalize** a denominator means to rewrite the expression so the denominator has no radicals. Multiply the top and bottom by a radical that makes the bottom a perfect square (or cube). Since $\frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}} = 1$, the **value doesn't change** — only its form.

  title="Square-root denominators"

## Two-term denominators: use the conjugate

Multiplying $\sqrt{3} + 1$ by $\sqrt{3}$ gives $3 + \sqrt{3}$ — still a radical. Multiplying by its **conjugate** $\sqrt{3} - 1$ uses the difference of squares from 10.4, and the radical disappears.

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="Conjugate"
  problem="Rationalize: $\;\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{5} - 1}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{6}{\\sqrt{5} - 1}\\cdot\\dfrac{\\sqrt{5} + 1}{\\sqrt{5} + 1}$', why: 'Multiply by the conjugate over itself (that is, by $1$).' },
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{6(\\sqrt{5} + 1)}{5 - 1} = \\dfrac{6(\\sqrt{5} + 1)}{4}$', why: '$(\\sqrt{5} - 1)(\\sqrt{5} + 1) = 5 - 1$.' },
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{3\\sqrt{5} + 3}{2}$', why: 'Reduce: $6$ and $4$ share a factor of $2$.' },
  ]}
/>

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="Radicals on top and bottom"
  problem="Rationalize: $\;\dfrac{\sqrt{3} + 1}{\sqrt{3} - 1}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{(\\sqrt{3} + 1)(\\sqrt{3} + 1)}{(\\sqrt{3} - 1)(\\sqrt{3} + 1)}$', why: '' },
    { show: '$= \\dfrac{3 + 2\\sqrt{3} + 1}{3 - 1} = \\dfrac{4 + 2\\sqrt{3}}{2}$', why: 'The numerator is a square: $(\\sqrt{3} + 1)^2$.' },
    { show: '$= 2 + \\sqrt{3}$', why: 'Divide **both** terms by $2$.' },
  ]}
/>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Canceling across the radical">
$\frac{\sqrt{6}}{3} \neq \sqrt{2}$. The $3$ is outside the radical and the $6$ is inside; they don't cancel.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Multiplying only the denominator">
Multiplying the bottom by $\sqrt{3}$ and not the top changes the value. Always multiply by $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}}$, which is $1$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Using the radical for a two-term denominator">
For $\frac{6}{\sqrt{5} - 1}$, multiplying by $\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{5}}$ gives $\frac{6\sqrt{5}}{5 - \sqrt{5}}$ — still a radical below. Use the **conjugate**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Reducing only one term">
$\frac{4 + 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2 + \sqrt{3}$: divide **every** term of the numerator by $2$, not just one.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}$; use it in whichever direction helps.
- Rationalizing multiplies by a form of $1$, so the value is unchanged.
- One-term denominator: multiply by the radical that makes a perfect square (cube).
- Two-term denominator: multiply by the conjugate over itself.
