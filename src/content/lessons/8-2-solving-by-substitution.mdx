## The idea: replace a variable with what it equals

At a solution, $x$ and $y$ have the **same values in both equations**. So if one equation tells you $y = 2x - 1$, then in the other equation you may replace $y$ with $2x - 1$: you are swapping a quantity for something equal to it. The result has only $x$ in it.

The method:

1. **Isolate** one variable in one equation. Pick a variable with coefficient $1$ or $-1$ if you can — that avoids fractions.
2. **Substitute** that expression into the **other** equation, in parentheses.
3. **Solve** the one-variable equation.
4. **Substitute back** to find the other variable.
5. **Check** the pair in both original equations.

## When the variable disappears

Sometimes, after substituting, **both** variables cancel. What is left tells you which special case you have:

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="A true statement"
  problem="Solve: $\;\begin{cases} y = 3x - 2 \\ 6x - 2y = 4 \end{cases}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$6x - 2(3x - 2) = 4$', why: '' },
    { show: '$6x - 6x + 4 = 4 \\;\\Rightarrow\\; 4 = 4$', why: 'The $x$-terms cancel and what remains is **always true**.' },
    { show: '**Infinitely many solutions**: every point on $y = 3x - 2$.', why: 'The second equation is just the first one rearranged and doubled — the same line.' },
  ]}
/>

<StepReveal
  client:visible
  title="A false statement"
  problem="Solve: $\;\begin{cases} y = 2x + 1 \\ 4x - 2y = 3 \end{cases}$"
  steps={[
    { show: '$4x - 2(2x + 1) = 3 \\;\\Rightarrow\\; 4x - 4x - 2 = 3 \\;\\Rightarrow\\; -2 = 3$', why: 'The variable cancels and what remains is **never true**.' },
    { show: '**No solution**: the lines are parallel.', why: 'No pair $(x, y)$ can make $-2 = 3$ true.' },
  ]}
/>

## In a real context

Substitution is the natural method when a fact in the problem *already* gives one unknown in terms of the other: "the length is 4 more than the width", "three times as many adults as children".

  steps={[

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Substituting back into the same equation">
If you solve the first equation for $y$ and substitute into the **first** equation again, you get something like $2x - 1 = 2x - 1$ — true, but useless. Substitute into the **other** equation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Forgetting the parentheses">
$3x + 2(2x - 1)$, not $3x + 2 \cdot 2x - 1$. The whole expression replaces $y$, so the $2$ multiplies both of its terms.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Sign errors when isolating">
From $x - 3y = 5$: $x = 5 + 3y$ (add $3y$ to both sides), not $x = 5 - 3y$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Stopping after one variable">
$x = 2$ is not the answer to a system. Substitute back to find $y$ and give the ordered pair $(2, 3)$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Substitution works because, at a solution, each variable has the same value in both equations.
- Isolate (coefficient $\pm 1$ if possible) → substitute into the **other** equation → solve → substitute back → check.
- If the variables cancel: a true statement means infinitely many solutions; a false one means no solution.
- In word problems, phrases like "4 more than" or "three times as many" give an equation that is already solved.

**Next, in 8.3:** when no variable has a coefficient of $1$ or $-1$, substitution brings fractions. *Elimination* avoids them.
